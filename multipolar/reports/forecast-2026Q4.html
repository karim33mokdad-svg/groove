Multipolar · ensemble forecast

Primacy without preponderance

Four hundred simulated decades, run forward from the world as it stands on 28 October 2026. What the model says about who leads, who falls, what burns, and what it costs.

Baseline 28 Oct 2026 Horizon Q4 2036 Runs 400 × 40 quarters Actors 14, all autonomous Theatres 11

Read this first

This is model output, not a prediction. Every percentage below is the frequency of an outcome across 400 runs of one set of equations. It is a statement about what these assumptions imply when you follow them for ten years — not about what will happen.

The model is wrong in ways I cannot enumerate, because any model of international politics is. What it can do is force assumptions to be explicit and consistent, then show where they lead. Where its output conflicts with your judgement, your judgement may well be the better instrument — but the disagreement will now be about something specific.

Nobody plays a seat in these runs. There is no protagonist and no advice here about what any state should do.

01 The finding

The United States stays first, and almost never by enough to run the system

In 99% of runs the United States ends 2036 as the largest single concentration of usable power. In 83% of those same runs the system is multipolar — meaning no power holds the margin that would let it set terms. Those two facts are not in tension. They are the decade's central structural condition.

A pole, in this model, is a power within reach of the leader. The United States is first almost always; it clears the second-placed power by the 1.75× margin that would make the system unipolar in only 17% of runs. American primacy is real and it persists. American preponderance — the capacity to impose outcomes rather than contest them — mostly does not return.

The most counter-intuitive result is the one that does not appear: a Chinese overtake. Across 400 runs China ends ahead of the United States in none of them. That is a strong claim and it deserves the scrutiny it will attract — see §2 for what drives it and §9 for why it may be the most fragile output in the report.

02 Who is the superpower

Three concentrations of mass, one of which can actually decide

Power here is a composite: economic mass, military capability, technology base, and alliance reach — then discounted twice. Once for whether the state can spend its capability (cohesion and standing), and once for actorness: whether the polity can take a decision at all.

Why China does not overtake, in this model

Decomposing the index makes the mechanism legible, and it is not about output. China's economic component is roughly two-thirds of the American one and closing slowly. Its military component is about a quarter. Its alliance component is approximately zero — Japan alone carries more alliance mass than China does. The United States is not ahead because it produces more; it is ahead because it produces a great deal and the model counts the Japanese, European and other aligned economies as partially usable, and counts nothing for Beijing.

Against that, China holds the one advantage the others lack: it can take a decision. Its actorness discount is 0.91, against 0.71 for the United States and 0.61 for the European Union. A decade in which Washington is gridlocked and Brussels is deadlocked is a decade in which that gap matters more than the index suggests.

The European Union is the model's sharpest verdict

On raw mass the EU is China's equal. On usable power it ends level, and only because its cohesion and standing are high enough to partly offset a unanimity rule that costs it 39% of its weight before anything is decided. An earlier version of this model, without that discount, ranked the EU as a near-peer superpower leading the system a third of the time. That was the model flattering an entity that cannot act, and it was wrong.

Where to push back. Any single index of power is a weighting judgement, and a different defensible set of weights reorders the middle of this table. The components are reported separately for exactly that reason. The weighting is also what produces Russia at 2.5% — an economy-heavy index will always rank a nuclear power with a small GDP low, and you may think that understates what Moscow can do with what it has.

03 Who is the leadership

Instability concentrates exactly where the strain already is

The model does not attempt to psychoanalyse named individuals; that would be invented precision. It models offices and regime types: how many veto players stand between an executive and a decision, how long that executive expects to last, when the next contest falls, what the regime's legitimacy rests on, and how much policy survives a handover. Onto that it adds one well-supported behavioural regularity — leaderships that perceive themselves losing become risk-acceptant, while the same leaderships ahead turn cautious.

The model's clearest leadership finding is not about any one capital. It is that turnover clusters where economic strain, war stress and collapsed institutional trust already overlap — and that each turnover widens the distribution of everything downstream.

This matters for reading the rest of the report. A transition in a party-institutional system hands over a programme; in a personalist or clerical system it hands over a vacuum, and doctrine, risk appetite and alignments are all redrawn. Much of the variance in the ten-year outcomes traces back to which systems turned over and when.

What this cannot see. It has no view on who any successor would be, and makes none. It treats succession as a draw from a distribution whose width is set by the regime type. Real successions are specific people with specific beliefs, and the single most important variable in several of these cases is therefore one the model deliberately refuses to guess.

Taiwan: 39%, and why you should treat it sceptically

This is the model's most consequential single number and its most contestable. Published expert estimates for a Taiwan conflict this decade span roughly 5% to 50%, so 39% sits at the pessimistic end of a very wide distribution. The mechanism driving it is specific: the Strait starts at sustained grey-zone pressure, that pressure has no natural ceiling below armed incident, each incident carries a small chance of a rung, and ten years is a great many quarters for a small per-quarter hazard to compound. If you think the real ceiling on coercion-short-of-war is firmer than the model's — that both capitals are better at stopping before the line than the model gives them credit for — you should mark this number down, and you may be right.

Note also what the branch analysis says about that war: in runs where it happens, China's share of system power ends at 19.1% against 22.1% where it does not. In this model the Taiwan war is something China loses by fighting, whatever the military outcome.

Ukraine settles in fewer than a third of runs

The negotiation track running into Abu Dhabi in early November is in the baseline, and it helps: a live track roughly doubles the per-quarter chance of the fighting stepping down a rung. It is still not enough. The war comes fully to rest in 29% of runs; the median outcome is that it stays at acknowledged strikes and sustained operations, neither resolved nor escalated, for most of the decade. The front line itself barely moves — ending at 55% Russian-held against 58% today, with a very wide range on either side. This is the war-duration pattern asserting itself: conflicts that survive their first year end slowly, because the cheap settlements have already been refused.

The quiet ones are a finding too

Korea settles to coercive posturing in 88% of runs and the Himalayan frontier in 98%. The model is saying that frozen disputes with functioning deterrence and no live claim-pressing stay frozen, and that attention follows pressure rather than history. Kashmir is the case to watch here: 11% escalation looks low against that dyad's record of compressed, days-long crises, and I would treat it as the model under-reading a genuinely crisis-prone relationship.

The third row is the structurally interesting one. The decade does not produce two blocs and a scattering of irrelevance. It produces two blocs and a third group — India and the Gulf most consistently — that is wealthy, growing, and makes a policy of belonging to neither. On the model's numbers, that group's weight rises through the decade while both blocs' internal cohesion costs rise. Non-alignment is not a residual category here; it is a strategy that pays.

The oil price is the clearest good news in the report and the least surprising. Brent near $97 today reflects a war premium, and premia decay: the median run sees the price drift back to the high seventies as the Middle East theatres cool. Energy exporters lose the windfall that is currently funding them — Saudi Arabia ends the decade with the strongest economic index on the board anyway, at 103, because a decade at even moderate prices is still a decade of revenue.

Inflation is the channel through which all of this reaches ordinary politics, and it does not fully normalise: the median run ends at 3.3%, persistently above target, with a long right tail. That is the single most politically consequential number in this section. Sustained above-target inflation is the most reliable predictor of incumbent defeat in the historical record, in every electoral system, and it is doing a great deal of the work behind §3's leadership churn.

The trade index tells the structural story. It starts at 96 — already depressed by the energy shock and an effective US tariff rate near 18.5% — and ends at 88. It does not recover in the median run or anywhere near the optimistic tail. The decade's economic signature is not collapse; it is a permanent, moderate tax on exchange, levied by tariffs, chokepoint risk and the slow separation of two technology ecosystems.

Economic trajectories diverge sharply, and demography explains most of it. India ends at 103 and is the only major economy meaningfully above its baseline. The United States ends at 95, China at 93, the EU at 96, Japan at 95 — all modestly below, all for the same reason: working-age populations that are flat or shrinking, against debt service that is not. Russia ends at 86 and Iran at 78, which is what a decade of war economy and maximum pressure looks like when the adaptation that sanctions provoke can no longer keep pace with the damage.

07 The society

Strain concentrates, and then feeds back into the politics that caused it

The model carries two sociological measures: discontent, driven mainly by prices and war rather than by output, and institutional trust, which falls faster than it rebuilds. Both feed into approval, into the political capital a government has to spend abroad, and into the hazard of a leadership losing power outside the scheduled process.

The ordering is the finding, and it is almost exactly the ordering of war exposure. The four societies under most strain at the end of the decade are the four that spend it at or next to a war. Nothing else in the model — not relative decline, not trade fragmentation, not leadership turnover — moves these numbers as far as sustained conflict and the prices that come with it.

Legitimacy diverges much more sharply than discontent. Iran ends at 4 and North Korea at 4; Russia at 20 and Israel at 25. These are states whose capacity to assemble coalitions, win votes in international institutions, or make commitments others believe has been substantially spent. That is a real strategic cost and it is slow to repair. The European Union ends at 72, the highest on the board — a reminder that the thing the EU converts into power at a heavy discount is nonetheless a genuine asset.

Sustained protest appears in the median run 0.59 times across the whole system per decade, with a p90 of 2. Governments survive these episodes far more often than they fall. What the episodes reliably do is consume the political capital that foreign policy runs on — which is the main channel by which domestic strain becomes an international outcome.

The 2% figure for nuclear use took several passes to calibrate and the intermediate results are worth stating. An early version produced 25%, which is not defensible: eighty years of non-use is the strongest base rate in the entire model. A later version produced exactly 0%, which is equally indefensible — a decade containing active wars between nuclear-armed states does not carry zero risk, and a model that reports zero is hiding a tail rather than estimating it. The final figure is a continuous hazard calibrated against the realised risk distribution rather than against a round number. It is low because the historical record says it should be low, and non-zero because the record is not a guarantee.

The Iranian programme is the most interesting tail. Its median outcome is neither suppression nor breakout but partial reconstitution — an index of 51, from 26 today, with a wide range. Strikes degraded the infrastructure and bought years; they did not end the programme, and they strengthened the domestic argument for a deterrent. The roughly 440 kg of enriched uranium unaccounted for means capability is uncertain rather than demonstrably ended, and the model treats it that way. In the branch where the programme recovers toward threshold, nothing else in the system changes much — which is itself a finding, and an uncomfortable one.

The Ukraine result deserves emphasis because it cuts against intuition. A negotiated end to the largest land war in Europe since 1945 changes the system's power distribution, its economics and its alignments by very little. The war's effects have already been absorbed: the European rearmament decision is taken, the Russian economic reorientation has happened, the alignments have hardened. Settlement matters enormously to Ukrainians and Russians. On the model's numbers it does not reorder the system.

The Taiwan result cuts the other way and is the reason that theatre dominates this report. It is the only branch that moves system structure, the economy and the nuclear tail together.

10 The assumptions

Fourteen claims about how international politics behaves

These are not decoration. Each is implemented as a mechanism, each has an empirical literature, and several are contested — where they are, the model deliberately uses the conservative end of the estimated effect. A forecast built on the strongest claimed version of every disputed finding is a forecast built to be wrong.

11 The limits

Where this is most likely to be wrong

It cannot see discontinuity. No technological shock, no pandemic, no financial crisis, no climate event, no ideological movement. The decade 2026–2036 will contain at least one system-reordering surprise, and by construction it is not here. Every number in this report is conditional on nothing arriving from outside the model's categories — which is the one thing the historical record most reliably refutes.

Its power index is a judgement. The weights — economic mass 40%, military 32%, technology 14%, alliance reach 14% — are defensible and they are not the only defensible set. A military-weighted index ranks Russia far higher. A technology-weighted index ranks Taiwan and Japan higher. The components are reported separately so you can reweigh them.

Taiwan at 39% is probably the weakest number here. It sits at the pessimistic end of published expert ranges and it comes from compounding a small per-quarter hazard over forty quarters. If the real ceiling on grey-zone coercion is firmer than modelled, this figure falls substantially, and much of §4 falls with it.

China's non-overtake is the most load-bearing assumption. It follows from demography, debt, technology controls and the absence of alliance mass. Each is defensible; together they may compound into more pessimism about Chinese trajectory than the evidence supports. If you weight alliance mass lower — and there is a real argument that counting allies' GDP toward a power's own index flatters the United States — the gap narrows considerably.

It does not model the thing it is about. There are no people in this simulation. There are offices, indices and hazards. Real conflicts kill real people, displace them, and end their societies' futures in ways no index represents. The million displaced in Lebanon and the four thousand dead appear in this model as a tension value. That is the abstraction the method requires, and it is a serious one.

Method. 400 runs × 40 quarters from a baseline of 28 October 2026, built from public reporting. Every capital is autonomous: each enumerates its legal moves each quarter, scores them against its own interests, doctrine, escalation tolerance and red-line exposure, and acts. There is no player. Draws come from a seeded generator, so the whole ensemble is reproducible from seed 20261028.

Provenance. Baseline facts are from public reporting as of 28 October 2026: the Iran war and its April ceasefire, the confirmation of a new Iranian supreme leader on 9 March, the Lebanon war and its de jure April ceasefire, the Ukraine negotiation track and the Oleshky pause, the hung Israeli election of 27 October, the US midterms of 3 November, Brent near $97, IMF headline inflation near 4.7% for 2026. Capability figures are rounded open-source approximations and should not be cited.
